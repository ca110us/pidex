# Worth building: win team photos in specialty clinics first

**Bottom line: worth building, with conditions. Do not build it as a "horizontal photo app for everyone", and the first customer should not be a hospital.** The pain point is documented. Surveys across countries show that **45%–90% of clinicians use (mostly personal) phones to photograph patients**. The photos sit alongside personal albums, leave via WhatsApp, iMessage or email, and consent records are widely missing. Regulators have even publicly told hospitals to deploy a "secure clinical image transfer system". But hospitals already have a free capture route in Epic Haiku, and their procurement cycles run to months or even years, so "pay right away" is structurally out of reach. CompanyCam has proven something else: small teams will pay **$63–199** a month for "site photos filed by project", and the category can grow into a company with a **$2 billion valuation**. My recommendation is a **"horizontal engine, vertical entry"**. Keep the product's foundation industry-neutral, and pick as the first wedge **private dermatology, plastic surgery and wound-care clinics in the US with 3–15 physicians**. The fallback wedge is non-medical: disaster restoration and small property management. The value proposition rests on three things: photos no longer stay on personal phones, all photos of the same patient gather in one place automatically, and any photo can be found in seconds. AI (a vision model plus Jev) lowers the cost of organising, but it is not the reason to pay. Treat AI training data as an option for now: reserve room for it in the data model, but do not make it the business model in year one. The sections below test the idea point by point with JTBD, ODI, the Mom Test, Kano, Cagan's four big risks, the Sean Ellis 40% test and other frameworks. They end with a pricing hypothesis, the MVP scope, a validation plan with explicit stop conditions, and a naming direction.

## The pain is real and widespread, but hospital users have no budget

Clinical photography has had a "shadow IT" problem for more than a decade. A survey at a Melbourne hospital (n=134) found that **65% of respondents had taken sensitive medical images on a smartphone; of these, 24% had obtained no consent at all, 64% stored them on a personal device, and 82% had shared them** ([HCPLive](https://www.hcplive.com/view/clinical-photos-often-captured-on-smartphones-without-consent)). Among Canadian plastic surgeons, **89.1% had photographed patients on a phone, and 73% kept those photos mixed with personal photos** ([PMC4806754](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4806754)). In a 2023 multicentre UK dermatology project, every respondent used a personal device to take photos, and only 10% used a written consent form ([BJD 2023](https://academic.oup.com/bjd/article/188/Supplement_4/ljad113.392/7206970)). The most convincing buyer signal comes from regulatory cases. At NHS Lanarkshire in Scotland, 26 staff shared patient data in a WhatsApp group **more than 500 times**, including photos and videos. The ICO issued a reprimand and recommended deploying a secure clinical image transfer system, and the hospital's own defence was precisely that "we did not have a secure clinical image transfer system" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2023/08/03/nhs_ico_warning/); [Digital Health](https://www.digitalhealth.net/2023/08/ico-reprimands-nhs-lanarkshire-for-sharing-patient-data-via-whatsapp/)). In aesthetics, Spain's AEPD fined a clinic **€10,000** for publishing before-and-after photos without consent ([Enforcement Tracker](https://www.enforcementtracker.com/ETid-2462)). The conclusion is that the real unmet need is not "taking photos". It is compliant storage (separate from personal albums, with consent recorded per purpose), searchability and team sharing.

The efficiency gains from purpose-built tools have been quantified. In an emergency department, uploading an image to the EMR took an average of **26.3 seconds with an app, versus 96.3 seconds with a camera** ([PMC6693297](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6693297/)). At a Level I trauma centre, the time from capture to filing fell from **14.9 hours with the camera method to 1.8 minutes** ([JMIR 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2561326X24000441)). One key gap must be pointed out, though: **no study yet measures how many minutes are spent "finding old photos"**. "Fast retrieval", one of the core pain points in the user's original idea, is still a reasonable guess as far as the evidence goes, and it needs to be measured in the validation phase.

The problem with the hospital route lies in the buyer structure. Physicians at Epic hospitals can already select a patient in Haiku/Canto, take a photo, choose a document type and save it to Media Manager, with no extra licence fee ([Houston Methodist Epic training document](https://it.houstonmethodist.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Epic-Mobile_020222_Capture-and-Review-Patient-Photos-and-Media.pdf); [Connect Care](https://manual.connect-care.ca/tools/Media-Manager)). Large academic centres such as Mayo simply build their own (PhotoExam captured 22,784 photos of 6,417 patients in 7 months) ([Appl Clin Inform 2019](https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/photoexam-adoption-of-an-ios-based-clinical-image-capture-applica)). On procurement, more than half of hospital executives say signing a new solution takes more than 3 months, and **35% say more than 6 months** ([HC Innovation Group](https://hcinnovationgroup.com/clinical-it/digital-health-innovation/news/21265559/survey-hospital-execs-struggle-with-digital-health-acquisition-strategy)). Usage frequency is also lower than intuition suggests: one large US health system uploaded about 32,000 photo documents across 54 specialties over 5 years ([PMC11950542](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11950542/)). That is about 6,400 a year, which is thin once spread across departments. Taken together, hospitals buy governance and compliance, not high-frequency use. The users are doctors and nurses, but the buyers are IT security, compliance, procurement and the value analysis committee. "Making users see at a glance that it is worth paying for" does not work in a hospital, because the people who see the value hold no budget.

## Scoring against ten frameworks: the pain is a painkiller, the horizontal positioning is a vitamin

The table below applies the main frameworks one by one to two versions: the user's original idea (a "hospital-first horizontal team photo app") and the version this report recommends (a "private specialty clinic wedge + horizontal engine").

| Framework | Core test | Hospital-first, for all industries | Recommended version: private specialty clinic wedge |
|---|---|---|---|
| JTBD (Christensen) | In what situation does the user "hire" what to get the job done | The job is clear ("capture, file and compare photos of the same patient across visits and across physicians"), but in Epic hospitals Haiku already does it | Same job; today it is done with personal phones, WhatsApp or scattered attachments in the EMR, so there is something to replace |
| ODI opportunity score = importance + max(importance − satisfaction, 0) ([ProductPlan](https://www.productplan.com/glossary/opportunity-scoring)) | Find outcomes with high importance and low satisfaction | "Putting photos into the chart" may already be over-served in Epic hospitals | "Find all past photos of a given patient's given body site in seconds" and "reduce the risk of photos stored on personal devices" are expected to score high; needs survey validation |
| Painkiller vs vitamin | Does something go wrong if it is not solved | A painkiller for the compliance lead, a vitamin for frontline physicians | Missing consent forms, photo leaks and before-and-after disputes put the risk directly on the clinic owner; leans painkiller |
| Frequency × intensity × budget (composite view, not a formal formula) | How often it happens × how severe the consequences × who pays | Frequency is scattered, intensity moderate, budget locked up by committees | Dermatology photographs at every visit, wound care at every dressing change; intensity is high, and the owner has their own budget |
| Mom Test ([mtlynch](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/)) | Ask about specific past events; judge success by commitments (time, introductions, payment) | Easy to collect compliments from physicians like "AI tagging is really cool", but these people do not pay | Can ask the buyer directly for a paid pilot or a letter of intent |
| Value Proposition Canvas | Does the value map hit the most painful pains and the most essential gains | Needs separate canvases for physicians, nurse managers, the CISO and compliance; hard to fit all at once | Buyer and user are only one step apart; one canvas is basically enough |
| Cagan's four big risks ([SVPG](https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/)) | Value, usability, feasibility, business viability | Value risk (the competitor is "Haiku is free") and business risk (cycle length, certification cost) are highest | Value risk is manageable; usability depends on completing "capture + filing + patient matching" within 10 seconds |
| Kano ([Learning Loop](https://learningloop.io/plays/kano-analysis)) | Separate must-be, performance and attractive (delighter) requirements | The hospital must-have list is long and expensive (EHR write-back, DICOM, VNA) | Must-haves are manageable: BAA, permissions, audit, consent, offline |
| Sean Ellis 40% ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)) | Share of active users who would be "very disappointed" ≥40% | Mixing many industries dilutes the score | A single high-expectation customer group reaches the bar more easily |
| Wedge scoring (synthesis of Tidemark, a16z and others) | Fragmented market, word-of-mouth channels, short cycles, compounding data, no strong vertical leader | Hospitals fail the short-cycle test; the contractor space already has CompanyCam | Fragmented, short cycle (about 60–90 days, unverified), highest data value |

Several frameworks reach the same conclusion: **the problem itself is a painkiller, but doing "horizontal + hospital + data business" all at once reads like "a solution looking for a problem"**. YC's Jared Friedman lists "working backwards from a technology to find a problem" as the most common mistake ([MIT notes](https://www.mit.edu/~edchen93/posts/how-to-get-and-evaluate-startup-ideas/)). Superhuman's experience shows that focus alone can produce PMF: **simply narrowing the survey to the right user profile raised the "very disappointed" share from 22% to 33%**, and about three quarters of iteration then took it to 58% ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)). For horizontal products, intent-based search channels often fail. CompanyCam found that Google Ads "never worked well", because contractors do not search for the category; growth came from demonstrating the product on social media and in trade communities ([Frontlines](https://www.frontlines.io/construction-tech-gtm-channels/)). Nobody searches for "team photo management", so demand can only be created through demos within specific industries.

That said, "horizontal" has its logic and should not be dismissed. Horizontal tools do have a proven path to success. Airtable started with a small group of tinkerers, found the use cases that grew on their own, "built hundreds of templates", and always targeted acquisition by use case ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/airtables-path-to-product-market-fit-lessons-for-building-horizontal-products/); [Decibel](https://www.decibel.vc/articles/plg-pqls-and-shortening-the-time-to-a-ha-at-airtable)). Fulcrum's early vertical home inspection product failed; after pivoting to a general field data collection platform, it reached 47,000 organisations by 2018 ([Fulcrum](https://www.fulcrumapp.com/blog/origins-of-fulcrum/); [Fulcrum 2018](https://www.fulcrumapp.com/blog/2018-in-review/)). The right approach is to put "horizontal" in the architecture and the brand, not in customer acquisition.

I recommend private specialty team clinics as the first wedge, for four reasons. First, **this is the group with the ability to pay that sits closest to the user's original insight**. The pain is exactly the same: photos taken by several physicians need to gather under the same patient. But the buyer shifts from a committee to the clinic's partners, so the hospital problem of "the people who see the value have no budget" does not exist here. Second, **willingness to pay is visible**. Public prices for clinic-facing photo tools sit between $150 and $350 a month: CIM starts at $299/month and is explicitly aimed at dermatology ([Software Advice AU](https://www.softwareadvice.com.au/software/563607/Clinical-Image-Management)), RxPhoto is $210/month ([Mindbody](https://integrations.mindbodyonline.com/partners/rxphoto)), and AestheticsPro is $285–350/month ([Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/medical/aesthetics-pro-profile/)). Third, **compliance is a selling point that earns money, not just a cost**. Signing a BAA and completing a risk assessment can be done in days to weeks. A lean HIPAA programme built from scratch costs about **$15K–40K and takes 4–6 months**. A first SOC 2 Type II audit costs about **$35K–80K and takes 9–14 months**, and can wait until larger customers come into view ([Topflight](https://topflightapps.com/ideas/hitrust-vs-soc2-vs-hipaa/); [Topflight HIPAA](https://topflightapps.com/ideas/hipaa-compliance-year-one/); [ComplyJet](https://www.complyjet.com/blog/soc-2-for-startups)). This set of trust materials can be reused later for hospitals, insurers and enterprise customers. Fourth, **the data value is highest**, as discussed later.

Pure aesthetics is deliberately avoided here. Aesthetics shows the clearest willingness to pay, but the space is full of products built as "before-and-after marketing galleries + EMR bundles", while this product's differentiation is in **team collaboration and case retrieval**, not marketing. Wound care can be included, but we must acknowledge that Swift Medical (about $48M+ raised, claiming coverage of more than 4,000 facilities in North America) does measurement and reimbursement documentation. Do not compete with it head-on in measurement features ([HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2024/01/29/swift-medical-secures-8m-to-power-ai-driven-wound-care/); [Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20230601005449/en)). Geographically, start with the US; the next stop is the UK. The UK IMI guidance defines clinical photographs as part of the health record and separates "consent to capture" from "consent to later uses" ([IMI](https://www.imi.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/NG_Mobile_Devices_2_0.pdf)). If the product meets IMI's auditable standards item by item, it has a chance to enter NHS departments that "ban personal phones but provide no alternative". The EU comes later, because GDPR Article 9 classes health data as a special category, and data residency is also involved ([GDPR Art. 9](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/)).

The fallback wedge is non-medical SMBs: disaster restoration, and move-in and move-out inspections in small property management. It was chosen because there is no HIPAA burden, true self-serve purchase is possible, and the structure of "many people taking photos many times, with photos accumulating in the same case" is identical to medicine. The research notes' scores for pain and willingness to pay in these industries are inferred, without direct evidence, so it is the fallback, not the first choice. Head-on competition should be avoided with contractors (CompanyCam), auto repair inspection (AutoVitals, from $219/month per shop), home inspection (Spectora) and insurance claims (CCC, Tractable), because these spaces already have strong report-centric leaders ([CompanyCam Pricing](https://companycam.com/pricing); [AutoVitals](https://www.autovitals.com/?p=8778); [Capterra Spectora](https://www.capterra.com/p/157144/Spectora/pricing/)).

## Making users see "worth paying for" at a glance: sell compliance and consolidation, win them over with AI

In B2B, "willingness to pay right away" can be roughly predicted from four signals: already spending money on an alternative; a clumsy workaround that can be timed; a compliance trigger that makes the status quo untenable; and a buyer who is the user or only one step away. All four hold in private specialty clinics. "Right away" is still the exception, so expectations should be set accurately. A 2026 study of 200 B2B products found that the **median free-trial-to-paid conversion is only 8%**, with a polarised distribution: 20% of products are below 2.5%, and 23% are above 25%. For trials that require a card, a "good" level is **25%–35%** ([Userpilot citing the ChartMogul/Poyar report](https://userpilot.com/blog/free-trial-conversion-rate/)). The recommendation is therefore a card-required or sales-assisted trial for a narrow customer group, aiming to land in the group with conversion above 25%, rather than broad freemium.

**Value proposition (in English, for the landing page):** *"Every photo of every patient — from every clinician — in one case, consented and findable in seconds."* Subheading for the buyer: *"Get patient photos off personal phones this week."* This wording puts the reason to pay on **compliance and team consolidation**, not on AI. The reason comes from the Kano model: AI auto-tagging and semantic search are delighters, and they gradually decay into performance and then must-be requirements ([Learning Loop](https://learningloop.io/plays/kano-analysis)). CompanyCam is well funded; once it catches up on search, this difference disappears. **The aha moment should be designed as: "The photo a colleague just took of the same patient shows up automatically in my case timeline; and the one from last year, I find in two seconds by typing 'left calf ulcer week 3'."** The activation metric is "at least 2 members file photos into at least 2 shared cases within 7 days". Team sharing is where the value lies; one person taking photos alone does not count.

**The MVP scope** is deliberately narrow. Must-have features: an in-app camera where you **select the patient or case first, then take the photo**, with wristband or barcode matching and name plus date of birth matching, and photos never go into the system photo library; **consent ticked separately per purpose** (four independent switches for treatment, teaching, marketing and AI training), which follows IMI's per-purpose consent principle and also answers the AEPD fine directly; a case timeline and side-by-side before-and-after comparison; structured tags preset by specialty (body site, view, stage), suggested by AI and confirmed by the physician in one tap; filtering, semantic search and search by image; role-based permissions, audit logs and offline sync; share links and PDF export for manual upload to the EHR. **Deliberately left out of the first version**: Epic/FHIR write-back, DICOM/VNA, wound measurement, skins for multiple industries, and data trading features. The hard usability targets are **a median capture-to-filing time of no more than 10 seconds and patient matching accuracy of ≥99%**. The data model stays industry-neutral from day one: "patient", "work order", "claim" and "housing unit" are just different names for the same case object. A second industry can then be brought on mainly by configuring templates, terminology and report formats, plus one or two integrations (following Airtable's template path: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/airtables-path-to-product-market-fit-lessons-for-building-horizontal-products/)).

**The pricing hypothesis** is anchored to two validated price points: CompanyCam's $63/$129/$199 per company plus $29 per seat ([CompanyCam Pricing](https://companycam.com/pricing)), and clinic photo tools at $150–350 a month. Charge per clinician (prescriber) seat, with unlimited support staff taking photos, because aesthetics EMRs also often price per physician, at $50–300 per physician per month ([OptiMantra](https://www.optimantra.com/blog/how-much-should-you-pay-for-a-medspa-emr-your-2025-pricing-guide)).

| Tier | For | Hypothetical price (annual billing, per month) | Key contents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solo | 1–2 physicians | $79/month | All core features, BAA included |
| Team | Up to 5 physicians | $249/month | Shared case space, per-purpose consent, audit export |
| Group | Up to 12 physicians | $499/month, $39/physician/month beyond that | Multiple sites, SSO, management reports |
| Enterprise / department | Hospital departments, chains | By quote | EHR integration, SOC 2 materials, data residency |
| Non-medical edition (fallback wedge) | Restoration and property teams | $49 / $129 / $249 | No BAA, report templates included |

There is no paywall on the BAA or AI features. Compliance is itself the selling point, and the cost of AI is low enough to ignore (see the next section). Prices should be validated with a Van Westendorp price sensitivity survey, and **only people who hold the budget should be asked** (clinic owners, operations managers), not individual physicians. Also ask about purchase likelihood, because this method measures price acceptance, not real willingness to pay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Westendorp%27s_Price_Sensitivity_Meter)).

## AI and Jev: perception is cheap, decisions need boundaries

Technical feasibility is not the bottleneck, and neither is cost. Assuming photos of about 1MP, auto-tagging with Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite or Claude Haiku 5.5 costs **about $0.14–0.18 per 1,000 photos**; generating image vectors with Gemini Embedding 2 costs about **$0.12 per 1,000 photos** ($0.06 in batch) ([Gemini API pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing); [Claude pricing](https://claude.com/pricing)). The real cost is in **human review**. Accuracy on specialty images also needs a sober look: the medical-specific CLIP (BiomedCLIP) performs clearly better zero-shot than general CLIP, but its absolute level is only moderate; RegionMed-CLIP's average AUC is about 77% ([BiomedCLIP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.00915); [RegionMed-CLIP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.05244)). Anthropic also states clearly that Claude "is not designed to interpret complex diagnostic imaging" ([Claude vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)). So AI is positioned as an **organising assistant**: suggesting tags, removing duplicates, detecting blur or sensitive areas such as faces, and semantic retrieval. It does not diagnose. Product copy and features must avoid diagnostic claims, so as not to fall under medical device regulation (the FDA/MDR boundary needs a separate legal opinion to confirm).

TypeSafe's Jev (released in early access on 15 September 2026) is a **"decision model" that does not generate text**. It takes text or JSON and answers typed questions defined by the developer (yes/no with a probability, picking one of several options, a score), with a confidence level attached. It costs $0.042 per million input tokens, output is free, and latency is 70–500 milliseconds ([The Rundown AI](https://www.therundown.ai/news/typesafe-jev-ai-decisions-software); [TrueFoundry](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/typesafe-ai-jev)). It fits **after** the vision model and expert input, making routing decisions on structured records. Typical questions include: "Should this record go into the expert confirmation queue?", "Does the consent scope cover export to a given dataset?", and "Which open case does this bulk-imported photo most likely belong to?" (a suggestion only; a person confirms the final answer). On records of about 500 tokens, the cost is about $0.02 per 1,000 decisions.

Its boundaries need to be stated clearly. First, **Jev cannot see images**, so it cannot replace the vision model. Second, "does not hallucinate" only means the output will not fall outside the allowed options; it does not mean the answer is correct. Its accuracy was measured against the averaged answers of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1, not against human experts, and its behaviour under high load has not been verified ([TrueFoundry](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/typesafe-ai-jev)). Third, at this product's volume the vision model itself is already cheap, so **Jev's cost advantage is economically marginal**; its real value lies in low latency, typed output and built-in confidence thresholds. Fourth, it is still in early access, which carries vendor risk. The recommendation is to put an abstraction layer behind the "decision layer" and use our own evaluation set to compare Jev, a small-model classifier and a cheap LLM with structured output side by side, then use whichever is most accurate.

## Training data is an option, not the business model for year one

The idea is attractive. Expert data is expensive: Mercor's physician experts earn **$130–180** an hour, and radiology goes up to about **$375–400** ([Mercor job listing](https://work.mercor.com/jobs/list_AAABndgW-_AC7W-RiLNN4qDm); [Ben's Bites citing the FT](https://news.bensbites.com/posts/56252-a-profile-of-mercor-which-pays-2m-per-day-to-30k-experts-training-ai-models-at-95-per-hour-on-average-with-roles-like-radiologists-earning-up-to-375hour)). Quotes for custom medical imaging datasets range from about **$40K to over $300K** ([LXT](https://www.lxt.ai/datasets/medical-imaging/)). Labels produced as a by-product of the workflow have been shown to train clinical-grade models: Campanella et al. used only the diagnoses in pathology reports as labels and reached **AUC >0.98** on 44,732 slides ([PMC7418463](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7418463/)). There is a ready analogy too: the medical photography platform True-See is using its 2 million colour-calibrated photos to build wound and dermatology training datasets with UC Irvine ([MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/true-see-teams-uc-irvine-ai-medical-photographs)). Regulation also points in a favourable direction: Article 10 of the EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to document data provenance, the original purpose of collection and the labelling process ([AI Act Art. 10](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/10/)), and the consent, photographer credentials and clinical context this product records at the moment of capture are exactly those fields.

But it cannot be the business model in year one, for four reasons. First, **money for expert data flows mainly to frontier labs for text RLHF and evaluation**. Surge's 2024 revenue exceeded $1 billion and Scale's was about $870 million ([Reuters via TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/news/reuters.com%2C2025%3Anewsml_L6N3SX0O9%3A0-scale-ai-s-bigger-rival-surge-ai-seeks-up-to-1-billion-capital-raise-sources-say/)), and that revenue does not come from selling image assets. Price discovery for image data is also poor: Mayo worked with 16 companies over two years, and its total data revenue was **under $5 million** ([STAT 2020](https://statnews.com/2020/06/03/mayo-clinic-patient-data-fuels-artificial-intelligence-consent-concerns)). Second, **workflow labels are noisy**. Oakden-Rayner's review of ChestX-ray14 found that positive predictive values were mostly **10–30 percentage points lower** than the documentation claimed ([AuntMinnie](https://www.auntminnie.com/imaging-informatics/artificial-intelligence/article/15624523/study-public-image-datasets-may-have-qc-issues)). There is also the "hidden stratification" problem: a model may learn an accompanying feature such as "chest drain" and perform worse on the key subgroup without a drain ([arXiv 1909.12475](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.12475)). Third, **legal thresholds**. HIPAA Safe Harbor de-identification requires removing "full-face photographs and any comparable images" ([eCFR 164.514](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.514)), so many dermatology and plastic surgery photos can only go the expert determination or patient consent route. Under GDPR, pseudonymised data is still personal data. Fourth, **reputational backlash**. Australia's OAIC eventually found that the I-MED and Harrison.ai collaboration had been sufficiently de-identified and took no action, but only after a round of public uproar ([RSM](https://www.rsm.global/australia/insights/what-health-organisations-can-learn-i-med-privacy-case)). The ICO found the DeepMind and Royal Free collaboration unlawful ([SCL](https://www.scl.org/6953-ico-ruling-on-royal-free-and-google-deepmind-trial/)). If a clinic suspects a vendor of "selling patient photos", the sale fails on the spot.

So in year one the approach is **reserve it in the design, do not switch it on commercially**. From day one the data model records: per-purpose consent, the photographer's identity and credentials, device and location, structured choices within the case (rather than free text), and accompanying features that could become "shortcuts" (for example, whether a dressing or device is present). In addition, a small sample goes through independent expert review to measure the noise rate. Commercially, AI training can only be a separate clause that **customers actively opt into**: the customer is the data controller, revenue is shared, and it is never on by default. Rather than selling datasets directly, a safer route is to be a "capture-side data engine": export COCO/YOLO formats, or connect to Labelbox and Encord, positioning upstream of these labelling tools rather than competing with them. The data route in non-medical industries (restoration and property damage photos) carries much lower legal risk and can serve as the first testing ground for the data business. The AI Act's high-risk obligations have reportedly been postponed to December 2027 and August 2028 ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/news/digital-omnibus-ai-act)), and that window allows the core product to be made solid first.

## Twelve-month validation plan, with stop conditions at every stage

| Stage | Timing | Actions | Pass criteria | Stop / pivot conditions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 Problem validation | Weeks 0–4 | Interview 30 people following the Mom Test (20 owners and operations managers from the main-wedge clinics, 10 from the fallback wedge); do time-and-motion observation at 3 clinics, **measuring how many minutes it takes to find old photos** | ≥60% can describe a specific event in the last 90 days (photos on a personal phone, could not find a photo, missing consent) and their current clumsy workaround; ≥5 sign a letter of intent or agree to a paid pilot | <30% have a specific event; or most think the EMR photo module is already good enough; or there are no commitments at all. In that case pivot to the fallback wedge. **If neither wedge passes, stop the whole project** |
| 1 Solution validation | Weeks 5–12 | Build a clickable prototype and a semi-manual (concierge) MVP; run paid pilots with 5–8 clinics at a founding price of $99/month; run a Van Westendorp survey in parallel | ≥5 paying; ≥60% of teams reach the activation bar (≥2 people, ≥2 shared cases within 7 days); median capture-to-filing ≤10 seconds, patient matching ≥99% | Fewer than 3 paying; or activation <30% after 4 weeks; or the midpoint of the buyer's acceptable price range is below $100/month (for a 5-physician practice) |
| 2 PMF | Months 4–9 | Run the Sean Ellis survey on ≥40 active users (≥2 uses within two weeks); following the Superhuman method, spend half the roadmap strengthening what power users love most and half removing obstacles for "somewhat disappointed" users | "Very disappointed" ≥40% in the core customer group; card-required trial-to-paid ≥25%; 3-month customer retention ≥90% | Still <25% after two rounds of iteration; or trial-to-paid <10% |
| 3 Data option | Months 9–12 | Launch the opt-in data clause; measure label noise on the review sample; approach 3–5 vision AI developers | ≥50% of customers sign; noise rate documented; ≥1 buyer signs a letter of intent | Sign-up rate <30% or no buyer interest: **shelve only the data business, do not cut the core product** |

The logic of these gates is to validate value risk and business risk first and leave technical feasibility for last, consistent with Cagan's ordering ([SVPG](https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/)). Two further risks need tracking. One is how much weight the objection "Haiku is good enough" really carries in private clinics: the research found no satisfaction data on EMR photo modules in private clinics, so it should be asked directly in stage 0. The other is that CompanyCam has already opened up the UK market and keeps expanding horizontally; if it enters the non-medical fallback industries, it will squeeze the room for the fallback wedge ([Silicon Prairie News](https://siliconprairienews.com/2025/11/companycam-reaches-unicorn-status-with-2-billion-valuation-a-first-for-a-nebraska-startup/)).

The recommendation is to choose the main brand from **Keepsight** and **Tracewell**. Keepsight is closer to the value clinic owners can feel directly (photos are kept and can be found); Tracewell is closer to the long-term story of data provenance. Before making it final, search Class 9 (software) and Class 42 (SaaS) at the USPTO, EUIPO and UKIPO, and for the medical direction also Class 44; at the same time, check the .com domain, App Store name clashes and similar trademarks within the industry. All the registrability assessments above are only first impressions and cannot replace a search.

## Conclusion

The most important correction from this assessment is that **"horizontal" and "hospital" are not wrong directions; they are just in the wrong place**. Horizontal belongs in the data model and the brand. Hospitals belong in the second stage, to be entered once the HIPAA and SOC 2 trust materials and EHR integration capability are ready. The truly valuable part of the original insight is "photos taken by many people automatically gather into the same case", and it can be monetised directly in private specialty team clinics, because there the people who see the value also hold the budget. One more point runs against intuition: AI is the cheapest and most easily copied layer of this product. The moat comes from three things that are not very "AI": structured choices at the moment of capture, consent recorded per purpose, and complete provenance records. These three things are at once the reason to pay, the compliance selling point and the foundation of a future data asset.

So the next step is not writing code. It is spending 4 weeks on 30 interviews and 3 on-site timing studies, to answer the two questions where the evidence is currently weakest: will clinics pay for "team consolidation", and how much time does "finding old photos" really take. If both hold, move into paid pilots with the MVP and pricing above. If neither the main wedge nor the fallback wedge holds, stop decisively.
